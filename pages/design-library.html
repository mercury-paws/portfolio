01 / How I think

Principles behind the interface

These notes use player-facing pages, including World of Tanks (WoT), as a practical lens. I use them to think about attention, understanding, and the next step in a user’s journey.

01

Player Perception

Players move through Eye → Brain → Conclusion → Decision. A page must first catch attention, then create order, then prove relevance, then make action obvious.

Eye → Brain → Conclusion → Decision

How I apply it — Player Perception
Eye
Give the first screen one focal point: a clear headline, a useful visual, and enough space to understand the offer.
Brain
Use familiar patterns, grouped content, and a reading order that distinguishes the main message from supporting details.
Conclusion
Explain who this is for and what they gain before introducing complex mechanics.
Decision
Keep the next action visible and specific. On long pages, repeat it where it becomes relevant.

My check: What should someone notice first, understand second, and do next?

02

Scanning Behavior

Most players scan before they read. Headings, visuals, numbers, rewards, and CTAs must communicate the page even without full sentence reading.

Make the page work at a glance

How I apply it — Scanning Behavior
Visible anchors
Bring dates, numbers, benefits, and actions out of dense paragraphs. Use short labels and meaningful headings.
Reading patterns
Consider F-like scanning in text-heavy content and Z-like paths in simple landing layouts as useful possibilities, then check the actual reading order.
A quick test
Read only the headings and buttons. The offer and the next step should still make sense.

My check: Can someone understand the main offer without reading every sentence?

03

Cognitive Load

Content becomes hard when line length, paragraph density, weak hierarchy, low contrast, inline complexity, and missing anchors overload the player.

Reduce the effort of understanding

How I apply it — Cognitive Load
Line length & density
Keep long explanations in a comfortable text column. Break dense passages into short paragraphs, steps, or lists.
Hierarchy & contrast
Give headings distinct roles and keep text legible over images or moving backgrounds.
Complexity & anchors
Separate dates, conditions, links, and rewards into clear groups. Give each section one useful visual or textual anchor.

My check: Am I asking the reader to do work that the layout could do for them?

04

Information Hierarchy

Use the inverted pyramid, clear heading outline, strong lead-ins, and one concept per paragraph. The layout should decide what the player understands first.

Value first. Details when needed.

How I apply it — Information Hierarchy
Inverted pyramid
Start with the benefit and required action, followed by any relevant deadline. Then explain rewards, conditions, and exceptions.
Heading outline
Make headings form a clear story. A lead-in should explain why a section matters, without simply repeating its title.
One concept at a time
Give dates, rewards, participation steps, and limitations their own space instead of mixing them into a single paragraph.

My check: Does the most important information also look the most important?

05

Player Journey

Map every page as Hook → Orient → Engage → Decide → Act. Every section should have a role in moving the player toward action.

Hook → Orient → Engage → Decide → Act

How I apply it — Player Journey
Hook
Catch attention with one relevant visual and a short, benefit-focused headline.
Orient
Explain what it is, who it is for, when it happens, and what participation involves.
Engage
Show the benefits, steps, or progression in clear, scannable groups.
Decide
Make the value and conditions easy to compare. Remove unnecessary friction.
Act
Use an explicit action such as “Claim Your Reward” or “Join the Event”, with the necessary context nearby.

My check: Does every section have a job in the journey?

06

Behavioral Psychology

Use reciprocity, consistency, scarcity, authority, social proof, Fogg’s model, Hick’s Law, Miller’s Law, loss aversion, and progressive disclosure to make WoT pages clearer, more motivating, and easier to act on.

A reason to act. An easy path. A clear prompt.

How I apply it — Behavioral Psychology
Reciprocity
Make a real gift or benefit clear, without disguising its conditions.
Commitment & consistency
Show how a small step connects to meaningful progress and what comes next.
Scarcity & loss aversion
Explain genuine deadlines and limitations clearly, close to the action they affect.
Authority & social proof
Use credible sources and specific, verifiable examples instead of generic popularity claims.
Fogg’s Behavior Model
Check that motivation, ability, and a prompt meet: a reason to act, an easy path, and a visible invitation.
Hick’s Law
Group choices and prioritize the main action so competing options do not overwhelm the decision.
Miller’s Law
Chunk related information into meaningful groups. Treat memory limits as a reason to simplify, not a fixed number of menu items.
Progressive disclosure
Keep core value and essential conditions visible. Put supporting explanations in expandable details.

My check: Is the design helping someone make an informed choice?
